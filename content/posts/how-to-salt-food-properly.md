Ask most home cooks when they salt and the answer is "at the end, to taste". That is one decision, made once, at the point where it has the least influence on anything except the surface.

Salt is doing four different jobs, and they are made at four different moments.

## Decision 1: when

This is the decision that changes texture rather than flavour, and it is the one most often skipped.

Salt on the surface of meat draws moisture out by osmosis. That brine then dissolves surface proteins and, given time, is reabsorbed, carrying salt inward. The meat ends up seasoned through rather than seasoned on top, and it holds moisture better under heat.

The timing rule follows directly from how long that cycle takes:

| Food | When to salt | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Thick cuts of meat | 12-24 hours ahead | Full absorption cycle completes |
| Thin cuts, chicken pieces | 45 min - 4 hours ahead | Shorter distance to travel |
| Any meat | Immediately before cooking | Acceptable, but surface only |
| Any meat | 5-30 min before cooking | The worst window, avoid |
| Vegetables for roasting | Just before the oven | Surface moisture blocks browning |
| Eggplant, cucumber | 30-60 min, then blot | Drawing water out is the point |

That fourth row is the trap. Five to thirty minutes is long enough for salt to pull moisture to the surface and not long enough for it to be reabsorbed, so you cook wet meat that steams instead of searing. Either well ahead, or right now. Not in between.

:::callout The practical version
Salt the meat when you take it out of the fridge to plan dinner, not when the pan is already hot. That single change in habit does more than any technique in this article.
:::

## Decision 2: which salt

This one is not about flavour, whatever the packaging says. Sodium chloride is sodium chloride, and the trace minerals in expensive salts are present in quantities you cannot taste dissolved in a stew.

It is about density. Crystal shape determines how much salt fits in a spoon, and the spread is large:

:::stat ~2x | Difference in salt by weight between a teaspoon of table salt and a teaspoon of flaky salt

- **Table salt** — dense, fine cubes. Roughly 6g per teaspoon. Dissolves fast, easy to overshoot.
- **Kosher / coarse salt** — larger irregular crystals. Roughly 3 to 5g per teaspoon depending on brand.
- **Flaky finishing salt** — hollow pyramids. Roughly 3g per teaspoon, and it stays crunchy on the surface.

Which is why a recipe calling for "one teaspoon of salt" is genuinely ambiguous, and why measuring by weight is not pedantry. If a recipe was written with table salt and you use coarse salt by volume, you are under-seasoning by nearly half.

Pick one salt for cooking, use it for everything, and learn what a pinch of it does. Keep a flaky salt for finishing, where the crunch is the point.

## Decision 3: how it is distributed

Salt added at the end sits in the liquid. Salt added at each stage is absorbed into each component.

Take a simple tomato sauce. Salting only at the end gives you salted liquid around bland onions. Salting the onions as they sweat, again when the tomatoes go in, and adjusting at the end gives you seasoned onions in seasoned sauce, using the same total quantity.

> The same amount of salt, added in three moments instead of one, tastes like more salt. Seasoning in layers is how restaurant food gets its depth without tasting salty.

Height matters too, and it looks like an affectation until you try it. Salting from twenty or thirty centimetres above the food spreads the crystals across the whole surface. Salting from five centimetres deposits them in a small circle, which is why one bite is sharp and the next is flat.

## Decision 4: how you taste

You cannot judge seasoning by tasting a component alone. A sauce tasted from the spoon is concentrated and hot, both of which distort your reading of it. Chilli, in particular, suppresses your ability to detect salt entirely.

- Taste on the carrier. If the sauce goes on pasta, taste it on a piece of pasta.
- Taste at serving temperature. Cold food needs more salt than the same food hot.
- Reset your palate. Water between tastes, and stop after three or four.
- Check acid before adding more salt.

That last point is the one worth internalising. Flatness and under-salting feel almost identical, and the fix is different. Add a few drops of lemon or vinegar first. If the dish comes alive, it needed acid. If nothing happens, it needed salt.

## What to change tomorrow

- [x] Salt meat hours ahead rather than minutes ahead
- [x] Pick one cooking salt and stop switching
- [ ] Add salt at every stage instead of only at the end

None of these is a technique. They are four decisions you were already making without noticing, made deliberately instead.
